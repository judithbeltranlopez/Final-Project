\begin{abstract}
In Ireland, whether a child can walk or cycle to school depends heavily
on where they live, something that has been described as a postcode
lottery. This study investigates whether the location of schools and how
easily they can be reached explains differences in active travel rates
across Ireland. We used data from the 2022 Census, the Department of
Education, and OpenStreetMap to measure school accessibility for over
16,000 small areas across Ireland. Two methods were used to measure
accessibility-kriging, which estimates the density of school places
across space, and r5r, which calculates how many school places can be
reached within a given walking or cycling time using the real road
network. We then used Bayesian Beta regression to model the relationship
between accessibility and the proportion of children walking or cycling
to school.
\end{abstract}

\section{2 Data}\label{data}

\subsection{2.1 School Data}\label{school-data}

The school data used in this study were obtained from the Department of
Education and Youth's \emph{Data on Individual Schools} collection. This
source provides school-level information for individual schools in
Ireland, including enrolment figures. For this analysis, the 2024/25
mainstream school dataset was used because it provides recent
school-level enrolment information together with school location
coordinates.

The school dataset was used to construct the main spatial input for the
school accessibility analysis. Each record represents an individual
school and includes information such as school location, school latitude
and longitude, and enrolment. In this study, enrolment was used as a
proxy for the number of available school places. The geographic
coordinates were used to convert each school into a spatial point,
allowing the school data to be linked with Small Area boundaries and
used in the kriging analysis.

The key variables extracted from the school dataset were:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  school latitude;
\item
  school longitude;
\item
  enrolment per return.
\end{itemize}

Schools with missing latitude or longitude were excluded because they
could not be represented spatially. Schools with missing, zero, or
invalid enrolment values were also excluded because enrolment was
required for the school-place accessibility measure. Enrolment was then
log-transformed before spatial interpolation, as the raw enrolment
distribution was positively skewed and contained some very large
schools.

All school coordinates were initially provided as latitude and longitude
and were converted into spatial point data using the WGS84 coordinate
reference system. The points were then transformed to the Irish
Transverse Mercator projection, EPSG:2157, so that spatial distances and
areas could be calculated in metres. This projected coordinate system
was used consistently throughout the spatial analysis.

Where multiple school records shared identical coordinates, these points
were aggregated by taking the mean log-enrolment value. This avoided
duplicated spatial locations in the kriging model and ensured that each
coordinate pair contributed only one observed value to the spatial
interpolation.

The cleaned school point dataset was used as the observed input for the
ordinary kriging analysis described in Section 3.1. The aim of this step
was not to count the number of schools within each Small Area directly,
but to estimate a continuous spatial surface of school-place intensity
across Ireland. This kriging-derived surface was later aggregated to
Census Small Areas and normalised by the population aged 3--18 to
produce the final school place density variable.

\subsection{2.2 Census Data}\label{census-data}

Census data were obtained from the Central Statistics Office (CSO),
using the Small Area Population Statistics (SAPS) and settlement-level
tables from Census 2022 and Census 2016. These data were used to
describe travel behaviour to education and to provide demographic and
spatial context for the analysis.

The main census outcome of interest was travel mode to education.
Settlement-level aggregated tables were used to obtain the proportion of
people travelling to education or childcare by different modes of
travel. For Census 2022, the relevant table was
\texttt{SAP2022T11T1TOWN22}, which provides travel-to-education data for
Towns/Urban Areas. This table combines travel to all forms of education
and childcare and covers approximately 800 settlements. For comparison
with the previous census, the 2016 table \texttt{SAP2016T11T1SET}
provides equivalent settlement-level data for all forms of education
combined. An additional 2016 table, \texttt{E6013}, provides
travel-to-education data separated by different stages or periods of
education, but for a smaller set of settlements.

These tables were accessed through the CSO's PxStat database. They can
be downloaded manually from the CSO website or programmatically in R
using the \texttt{csodata} package. Accessing the tables through R
ensures that the data extraction is reproducible and that the table
codes used in the analysis are clearly documented.

The settlement-level census data were linked to spatial boundary data
using CSO Towns/Urban Areas boundaries. For the 2022 analysis, the
relevant spatial geography is the CSO Towns/Urban Areas 2022 layer.
These boundaries correspond to the Built Up Areas geography used in
Census 2022. Built Up Areas were introduced as a new urban geography and
are based on clusters of urban development. Their boundaries are
designed to be compatible with Census 2022 Small Areas, which supports
consistent spatial analysis.

The census travel tables were used to calculate the proportion of people
travelling to education by active modes. Active travel was defined as
walking or cycling. The active travel percentage was calculated as:

The active travel percentage was calculated as:

\[
100 \times \frac{Walking + Cycling}{Total - Not\ stated - Not\ applicable}
\] This measure was used to describe variation in active travel to
education across settlements and to provide contextual evidence for the
later Small Area-level modelling. The settlement-level data were
particularly useful for understanding broader urban patterns, while the
Small Area SAPS data were used in the main statistical modelling because
they provide a finer spatial resolution and can be linked directly to
the school accessibility measures.

In addition to travel mode, Census 2022 SAPS data were used to derive
demographic covariates for the statistical model. These included age
composition, car availability, educational attainment, population aged
3--18 years, total population, and urban--rural classification. The
population aged 3--18 years was used to normalise the kriging-derived
school place measure, producing a school place density variable per
1,000 children. Urban--rural classification was used to restrict the
main modelling analysis to urban Small Areas, which were the focus
population of the study.

Overall, the census data provided three key components for the analysis:
the active travel outcome, demographic control variables, and spatial
boundaries for linking statistical data to geographic areas.

\section{3 Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{3.1 School Place Density:
Kriging}\label{school-place-density-kriging}

This section describes the construction of the school place density
variable used as the main spatial accessibility exposure in the
analysis. The aim was to estimate how the availability of school places
varies spatially across Ireland and to derive a Small Area-level measure
that could be linked to Census 2022 demographic and travel behaviour
data.

The input school data consisted of individual mainstream schools for the
2024/25 academic year, including school coordinates and enrolment
counts. Since school enrolment was used as a proxy for school place
availability, schools with missing coordinates or invalid enrolment
values were removed. Enrolment was log-transformed before interpolation
because the original enrolment distribution was positively skewed.

All spatial datasets were transformed to the Irish Transverse Mercator
coordinate reference system, EPSG:2157, so that distances and areas were
measured consistently in metres. Schools with identical coordinates were
aggregated by taking the mean log-enrolment value, avoiding duplicated
point locations in the geostatistical model.

Ordinary kriging was used to predict school place availability at
unsampled locations. Kriging is appropriate here because it uses the
spatial autocorrelation structure of the observed school data to
estimate values at locations where no school is directly observed. The
spatial dependence in log-enrolment was first assessed using an
empirical variogram. An exponential variogram model was then fitted and
used to generate kriging predictions.

Instead of predicting only at the centroid of each Census Small Area,
each Small Area was divided into a 3 × 3 internal grid. Prediction
points were created within the valid clipped grid cells using a
point-on-surface approach. Kriging predictions were obtained at these
internal points and then averaged to obtain a single predicted value for
each Small Area. This approach was used to better represent irregularly
shaped Small Areas and reduce dependence on a single centroid.

The raw kriging output was back-transformed from the log scale using the
exponential function. This produced a raw spatially interpolated
estimate of school place intensity. However, because Small Areas differ
in the number of children living in them, this raw value was normalised
by the Census 2022 population aged 3--18 years. The final school place
density variable was expressed as predicted school place intensity per
1,000 children aged 3--18:

\[
SchoolDensity_i =
1000 \times \frac{RawKrigedValue_i}{PopulationAged3to18_i}
\]

Higher values indicate greater predicted school place availability
relative to the local school-age population. Lower values indicate lower
predicted availability relative to potential local demand. This
normalised variable was used in the later statistical modelling as the
main school place accessibility exposure.

\subsection{3.3 Demographic Variables: ILR
Transformation}\label{demographic-variables-ilr-transformation}

Several demographic covariates used in this study are expressed as
percentages. These include age composition, household car availability,
and educational attainment. Although percentages are easy to interpret
descriptively, they require special treatment in regression modelling
because they are compositional variables. A composition is a set of
parts that together represent a whole. For example, the age-group
percentages in a Small Area represent the distribution of the local
population across age categories, while the car-ownership percentages
represent the distribution of households according to the number of cars
available.

The main issue with compositional variables is that their components are
not statistically independent. If one percentage increases, at least one
other percentage must decrease because the parts are constrained by a
fixed total. This means that raw percentages do not behave like ordinary
unconstrained regression predictors. Including all components directly
in a regression model can lead to perfect or near-perfect collinearity,
unstable coefficients, and misleading interpretations.

To address this issue, the demographic percentage variables were
transformed using the isometric log-ratio transformation. The ILR
transformation converts a composition with (D) parts into (D - 1)
real-valued coordinates. These transformed coordinates can then be used
in standard regression models because they are no longer constrained to
sum to a fixed total. In this analysis, the ILR-transformed variables
represent balances between groups of compositional parts rather than
isolated changes in individual percentages.

Exact zero values cannot be used directly in log-ratio transformations
because the logarithm of zero is undefined. Therefore, before applying
the ILR transformation, zero percentages were replaced with a very small
positive value. This allowed the transformation to be computed while
minimally altering the original data structure.

In this study, the ILR transformation was applied to demographic
variables describing the composition of urban Small Areas. The
transformed ILR coordinates were then used as covariates in the
statistical model instead of the original percentage variables. This
approach reduces collinearity between demographic percentages and
ensures that the regression model respects the relative nature of
compositional data.

\subsection{3.4 Statistical Model: Bayesian Beta
Regression}\label{statistical-model-bayesian-beta-regression}

The main statistical model was used to estimate the association between
school place accessibility and active school travel in urban Small
Areas. The outcome variable was the percentage of pupils travelling to
school by active modes, defined as walking or cycling. Since this
outcome is a proportion bounded between 0 and 1 after rescaling, a beta
regression model was used.

Beta regression is appropriate for this analysis because the response
variable represents a continuous proportion. Standard linear regression
would not be ideal because it could generate predicted values below 0 or
above 1 and assumes constant variance across the outcome range. In
contrast, the beta distribution is naturally defined on the open
interval ((0, 1)), making it suitable for modelling proportional
outcomes.

Before fitting the model, the active travel percentage was converted
from a percentage to a proportion. Since the beta distribution cannot
handle exact values of 0 or 1, the transformed response was adjusted
slightly so that all observations lay strictly between 0 and 1.

The model was fitted only to urban Small Areas, as these were the main

included using standardised ILR-transformed variables. These ILR
variables adjust for differences in age structure, car ownership, and
educational composition while avoiding the collinearity problems
associated with raw percentages.

The school accessibility variables were included using natural cubic
spline terms. This allowed the relationship between school accessibility
and active travel to be non-linear. This is important because the effect
of school place accessibility may not be constant across its full range.
For example, increases in accessibility may be strongly associated with
active travel at lower values, but the association may flatten at higher
values. Natural cubic splines allow this type of flexible relationship
while avoiding the assumption of a purely linear effect.

Two natural cubic spline terms were included in the model. The first was
\texttt{ns(krig\_value\_per\_1000\_3\_18,\ df\ =\ 3)}, which represents
school place density per 1,000 children aged 3-18. The second was
\texttt{ns(places\_density\_km2,\ df\ =\ 3)}, which represents predicted
school places per square kilometre. Both spline terms used three degrees
of freedom, allowing flexible but controlled non-linear relationships
with active school travel.

The first term represents school place availability relative to the
local school-age population. The second term represents spatial density
of predicted school places per square kilometre. Including both terms
allows the model to distinguish between accessibility relative to
population demand and the spatial concentration of school places.

The fitted model can be written as:

\[
y_i \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\mu_i, \phi)
\]

\[
\mathrm{logit}(\mu_i) =
\alpha +
\sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j Z_{ij}
+
f_1(K_i)
+
f_2(D_i)
\] where (y\_i) is the active travel proportion in urban Small Area (i),
(\(\mu_i\)) is the expected active travel proportion, and (\(\phi\)) is
the beta precision parameter. The terms (Z\_\{ij\}) represent the
standardised ILR demographic covariates. The function (f\_1(K\_i))
represents the natural spline for school place density per 1,000
children aged 3--18, and (f\_2(D\_i)) represents the natural spline for
school places per square kilometre.

Weakly informative priors were used for the model parameters. A normal
prior with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 was assigned to the
regression coefficients. This prior regularises the model by
discouraging unrealistically large effects while still allowing the data
to influence the posterior estimates. The intercept was assigned a wider
normal prior with mean 0 and standard deviation 2.5 on the logit scale.
The beta precision parameter, (\(\phi\)), was assigned an exponential
prior to ensure that it remains positive.

\subsection{3.5 Normalisation by School-Age
Population}\label{normalisation-by-school-age-population}

After estimating the raw kriging-derived school place intensity, the
measure was normalised by the local school-age population. This step was
necessary because the raw kriging value represents predicted school
place availability, but it does not account for the number of children
living in each Small Area. Areas with higher predicted school place
intensity may not necessarily have better accessibility if they also
contain a large number of school-age children.

To account for local demand, the raw kriged school place value was
divided by the population aged 3 to 18 years in each Small Area. This
age range was selected because it approximates the population
potentially requiring access to primary, post-primary, and related
school services. The resulting measure was then multiplied by 1,000 to
make the variable easier to interpret.

The final normalised school place accessibility measure was calculated
as:

\[
SchoolDensity_i =
1000 \times
\frac{RawKrigedValue_i}{PopulationAged3to18_i}
\]

where \texttt{RawKrigedValue\_i} is the raw kriging-derived school place
value for Small Area \texttt{i}, and \texttt{PopulationAged3to18\_i} is
the number of residents aged 3 to 18 in that Small Area.

Higher values indicate that an area has greater predicted school place
availability relative to the number of children aged 3--18 living there.
Lower values indicate that predicted school place availability is lower
relative to the local school-age population. Therefore, this variable
can be interpreted as a relative school accessibility measure rather
than a simple measure of school concentration.

This normalisation was important for the research question because
active school travel is likely to depend not only on the presence of
schools, but also on the relationship between school availability and
the local population of children. Without this adjustment, areas with
high absolute school place intensity could be overestimated as highly
accessible, even if they also have a large school-age population.

The variable `krig_value_raw` was retained as the unnormalised kriging prediction. The variable `krig_value_per_child_3_18` represents the predicted school place intensity per child aged 3–18. The variable `krig_value_per_1000_3_18` expresses the same measure per 1,000 children, making it more interpretable. In the final modelling dataset, `krig_value` was set equal to `krig_value_per_1000_3_18`, meaning that the main school accessibility variable used in the statistical model was the normalised measure.

```{r normalisation-summary, message=FALSE, warning=FALSE} id="hglpml"
final_df %>%
  summarise(
    n = n(),
    min_population_3_18 = min(pop_3_18, na.rm = TRUE),
    median_population_3_18 = median(pop_3_18, na.rm = TRUE),
    max_population_3_18 = max(pop_3_18, na.rm = TRUE),
    min_school_density = min(krig_value_per_1000_3_18, na.rm = TRUE),
    median_school_density = median(krig_value_per_1000_3_18, na.rm = TRUE),
    max_school_density = max(krig_value_per_1000_3_18, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
\end{verbatim}

For interpretation, this normalised variable should be understood as a
relative measure of school place availability. It does not represent the
exact number of school places physically located within each Small Area.
Instead, it represents the kriging-derived estimate of school place
intensity relative to the number of children aged 3--18 living in that
area.
