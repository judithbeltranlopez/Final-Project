\begin{abstract}
Active travel to school is shaped by the spatial relationship between
where children live, where schools are located, and how easily schools
can be reached. This study investigates whether school-place
accessibility is associated with differences in the proportion of
children walking or cycling to school across Ireland. Data from Census
2022, the Department of Education, and OpenStreetMap were combined to
construct school accessibility measures for Census Small Areas. Two
complementary accessibility measures were developed. First, a
kriging-based measure was used to estimate spatial variation in
school-place density across Census Small Areas. Second, a routing-based
travel-time accessibility measure was used to estimate the number of
school places reachable within walking and cycling time thresholds using
the road and path network. Bayesian beta regression was then used to
model the association between school accessibility and the proportion of
children travelling to school by active modes, adjusting for demographic
composition using isometric log-ratio transformed covariates. This
approach allows the relationship between school accessibility and active
school travel to be examined while accounting for spatial variation in
local population characteristics. The results provide evidence on
whether differences in school-place accessibility contribute to unequal
opportunities for active school travel in Ireland.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Active travel to school, particularly walking and cycling, is an
important part of everyday mobility for children and young people. It is
shaped not only by individual or household characteristics, but also by
the spatial organisation of schools, neighbourhoods, and transport
networks. Children are more likely to walk or cycle when schools are
located within a feasible distance and can be reached through safe and
connected routes. For this reason, school accessibility is an important
factor in understanding differences in travel behaviour to education.

In Ireland, access to nearby schools is not evenly distributed across
space. Some areas have a high concentration of school places within
short walking or cycling distances, while others have fewer nearby
options. This has been described as a form of ``postcode lottery'',
where opportunities for active school travel may depend partly on where
children live. However, this argument requires spatial evidence: it is
necessary to measure school accessibility at a local scale and examine
whether areas with better access to school places also have higher
active travel rates.

This study investigates the relationship between school-place
accessibility and active school travel across Ireland. The analysis
combines Census 2022 Small Area data, Department of Education school
enrolment and location data, and OpenStreetMap network data. Two
complementary measures of school accessibility are developed. First, a
kriging-based measure estimates the spatial distribution of school-place
density across Census Small Areas. Second, a routing-based travel-time
measure estimates how many school places can be reached within walking
and cycling time thresholds using the road and path network.

The main outcome is the proportion of pupils travelling to school by
active modes, defined as walking or cycling. Bayesian beta regression is
used to model the association between school accessibility and active
travel in urban Census Small Areas, while adjusting for demographic
composition, car availability, and educational attainment. This allows
the analysis to examine whether differences in school accessibility are
associated with differences in active school travel after accounting for
local population characteristics.

The objectives of this study are therefore to: construct local measures
of school-place accessibility across Ireland; compare kriging-based and
travel-time-based accessibility measures; estimate the association
between school accessibility and active school travel; and assess
whether spatial inequalities in school-place accessibility may
contribute to unequal opportunities for walking and cycling to school.

\subsection{Background}\label{background}

School travel is not only a question of individual choice. It is also
shaped by distance, school location, educational provision, and the
structure of the local transport network. Walking and cycling to school
are widely promoted because they can support healthier daily routines,
reduce traffic congestion, and contribute to lower transport-related
emissions. However, the opportunity to travel actively depends on
whether schools are located within a feasible distance and whether they
can be reached through suitable routes.

In Ireland, low levels of active travel to school are often discussed in
terms of behaviour change, with policy attention directed towards
encouraging pupils and families to walk or cycle. While behavioural
factors are important, this explanation can overlook the spatial
conditions that make active travel possible. If children do not have
access to nearby schools, or if available schools are not reachable
within reasonable walking or cycling times, then active travel may be
difficult regardless of individual preferences.

Recent research has highlighted the importance of educational provision
in shaping school travel patterns. Mancini argues that inequalities in
school provision and school access form part of the wider Irish
educational landscape (Mancini 2024a). Related work on adolescent active
transport in Ireland suggests that active travel does not simply follow
population size or density, but is strongly linked to the presence and
type of local school provision (Mancini 2025). This argument has also
been discussed in a public-facing RTÉ Brainstorm article, which
describes teenage active transport as a potential ``postcode lottery''
shaped by where young people live and what schools are available locally
(Mancini 2024b).

This study builds on that research context by measuring school
accessibility at a finer spatial scale. Instead of analysing only
settlement-level differences, it uses Census Small Areas to examine
local variation in school-place accessibility and active school travel.
By combining school enrolment and location data, Census 2022 travel
data, and OpenStreetMap network data, the study assesses whether areas
with greater access to school places also have higher proportions of
pupils walking or cycling to school.

\subsection{The Postcode Lottery Argument from the
Article}\label{the-postcode-lottery-argument-from-the-article}

The idea of a ``postcode lottery'' provides an important motivation for
this study. In the context of school travel, the term suggests that
opportunities for walking or cycling to school may depend partly on
where a child lives. If school places are unevenly distributed, then
children in some neighbourhoods may have nearby schools that are
reachable by active modes, while children in other areas may need to
travel longer distances or rely on motorised transport.

Mancini's work argues that active travel to school should not be
understood only as a matter of individual behaviour or family
preference. Instead, it is also shaped by the geography of school
provision. The RTÉ Brainstorm article summarising this research states
that disparities between areas are not simply explained by population
size, but are linked to differences in educational provision (Mancini
2024b). This is important because it challenges the assumption that low
levels of walking or cycling are mainly the result of unwillingness to
travel actively. In some places, active travel may be less feasible
because suitable school places are not located nearby or are not
accessible through the local transport network.

This argument is also connected to wider research on inequalities in the
Irish educational landscape. Mancini's study of unequal school provision
examines how the distribution and type of schools can create different
educational opportunities across communities (Mancini 2024a). Related
research on adolescent active transport in Ireland links school
provision with unequal access to walking and cycling opportunities,
showing that the spatial organisation of schools is relevant to
sustainability, mobility, and education policy (Mancini 2025).

In this study, the ``postcode lottery'' argument is treated as an
empirical question. Rather than assuming that school access directly
determines active travel, the analysis tests whether areas with greater
school-place accessibility also show higher proportions of pupils
walking or cycling to school. This is done by constructing two
complementary accessibility measures: a kriging-based measure of
school-place density and a routing-based travel-time measure of
reachable school places. By analysing these measures at Census Small
Area level, the study examines whether spatial inequalities in school
provision are associated with unequal opportunities for active school
travel in Ireland.

\subsection{Research Objectives}\label{research-objectives}

Working with official statistics presents important methodological
challenges for studying school travel behaviour at a detailed spatial
scale. Census and administrative datasets are usually released in
aggregated form, meaning that individual residential locations, school
destinations, and travel routes are not directly observed. This study
therefore combines spatial modelling, accessibility analysis, and Census
Small Area data to examine how school-place accessibility relates to
active school travel across Ireland.

The first objective is to map and analyse the spatial distribution of
schools across Ireland, using school location and enrolment data to
describe where school places are concentrated.

The second objective is to develop local measures of school
accessibility. This includes a kriging-based measure of school-place
density and a routing-based travel-time measure of the number of school
places reachable by walking or cycling.

The third objective is to describe school travel behaviour using Census
2022 data, focusing on the prevalence of active travel modes, defined as
walking and cycling, across Census Small Areas.

The fourth objective is to examine the relationship between school
accessibility and active school travel in urban Census Small Areas,
while adjusting for demographic composition, car availability, and
educational attainment.

Together, these objectives aim to assess whether spatial differences in
school-place accessibility are associated with unequal opportunities for
walking and cycling to school in Ireland.

\section{Data}\label{data}

\subsection{School Data}\label{school-data}

School data were obtained from the Department of Education and Youth's
2024/25 primary and post-primary school enrolment datasets (Department
of Education and Youth 2025b, 2025a). These datasets provide
school-level information for individual schools in Ireland, including
school location coordinates and enrolment figures. The 2024/25 datasets
were used because they provide recent enrolment information together
with latitude and longitude coordinates for each school. These
coordinates allowed schools to be represented directly as spatial point
data without requiring additional geocoding.

The school datasets were used to construct the spatial inputs for both
school accessibility measures used in this study. Each record represents
an individual school and includes its geographic location and enrolment
per return. Enrolment was used as a proxy for the number of school
places available at each location. Although enrolment does not measure
spare capacity directly, it provides a consistent measure of the
relative size of schools and the spatial distribution of school places
across the country.

The key variables extracted from the school datasets were school
latitude, school longitude, and enrolment. School records without valid
latitude or longitude coordinates were excluded because they could not
be represented spatially. Enrolment values were checked before analysis
to ensure that the school-place measure was based on valid pupil counts.
No schools were removed for missing, zero, or invalid enrolment values
after this checking step.

All school coordinates were initially provided in latitude and longitude
using the WGS84 coordinate reference system. The school points were then
transformed to the Irish Transverse Mercator projection, EPSG:2157. This
projected coordinate system was used because distances, areas, and
spatial operations are measured in metres, making it appropriate for the
kriging analysis and for linking school locations to Census Small Areas.

Where multiple school records shared identical coordinates, enrolments
were summed before analysis. This ensured that co-located schools, such
as junior and senior schools on the same campus, contributed their
combined enrolment to the school-place measure. Aggregating duplicate
coordinates in this way avoided duplicated spatial locations while
preserving the total number of pupils represented at each site.

The cleaned school point dataset was then used in two complementary
parts of the accessibility analysis. First, the school locations and
enrolment values were used as the observed input for the kriging-based
school-place density measure. This produced an area-level estimate of
school-place availability across Census Small Areas. Second, the same
school locations were used as destination points in the routing-based
travel-time accessibility analysis. In that analysis, school enrolment
was used to weight destinations so that accessibility was measured as
the number of school places reachable within specified walking and
cycling travel-time thresholds, rather than simply the number of schools
reachable.

This means that the school dataset contributed both a spatial density
measure and a network-based travel-time measure of school accessibility.
The kriging-based measure captures the broader spatial distribution of
school places, while the routing-based measure captures how many school
places can be reached through the actual road and path network within a
given travel time. Together, these two measures allow school
accessibility to be assessed from both a spatial-proximity perspective
and a practical travel-time perspective.

\subsection{Census Data}\label{census-data}

Census data were obtained from the Central Statistics Office (CSO). The
main census dataset used in the statistical modelling was the Census
2022 Small Area Population Statistics (SAPS) dataset (Central Statistics
Office 2023a). Census Small Areas were used because they provide a fine
spatial resolution and can be directly linked to the school
accessibility measures constructed from school locations, kriging
predictions, and routing-based travel-time calculations.

The main outcome variable was the proportion of pupils travelling to
school by active modes. Active travel was defined as walking or cycling.
The outcome was calculated from Census 2022 travel-to-education
variables at Census Small Area level, excluding records with no stated
mode of travel or where the question was not applicable. This produced
an active-travel percentage for each Census Small Area that could be
linked to the school accessibility measures and used as the response
variable in the statistical model.

Census 2022 SAPS data were also used to derive demographic and
population covariates. These included total population, population aged
3--18 years, age composition, household car availability, educational
attainment, and urban--rural classification. The population aged 3--18
years was used to normalise the kriging-derived school-place measure, so
that school-place availability was expressed relative to the number of
school-age children living in each Census Small Area. Urban--rural
classification was used to identify the urban Census Small Areas used in
the main modelling analysis.

In addition to the Census Small Area data used in the model,
settlement-level CSO tables were used for descriptive and contextual
analysis of travel-to-education patterns. For 2022, table
SAP2022T11T1TOWN22 provides travel-to-education and childcare data by
mode for Towns/Urban Areas (Central Statistics Office 2023b). For
comparison with the previous census, table SAP2016T11T1SET provides
equivalent 2016 settlement-level data for travel to education (Central
Statistics Office 2017b). Table E6013 provides 2016 travel-to-education
data separated by different periods of education for a smaller set of
settlements (Central Statistics Office 2017a). These settlement-level
tables were not used as the main modelling geography, but they provided
broader context for interpreting active-travel patterns across Irish
settlements.

Overall, the census data provided three key components for the analysis:
the active-travel outcome, the demographic and population covariates,
and the spatial units used to link travel behaviour with school
accessibility. The Census Small Area data formed the basis of the main
statistical modelling, while the settlement-level tables were used only
to support descriptive comparisons and contextual interpretation.

\subsection{Study Area}\label{study-area}

The study area was the Republic of Ireland. The analysis was conducted
at the Census Small Area level, using Census Small Area boundaries from
the 2022 national statistical boundary dataset (Tailte Éireann 2023).
Census Small Areas were selected as the main spatial unit because they
provide a fine geographical scale for linking population
characteristics, school accessibility, and travel behaviour. This scale
is more appropriate for studying school travel than larger
settlement-level geographies, because walking and cycling to school are
highly local behaviours.

Each Census Small Area was treated as a neighbourhood-level unit of
analysis. Census 2022 demographic and travel variables were linked to
these boundaries, while school accessibility measures were derived from
school locations and then summarised at the Census Small Area level. For
the kriging-based measure, school-place density predictions were
aggregated to Census Small Areas. For the routing-based travel-time
measure, Census Small Areas were used as the origins from which
reachable school places were calculated using the road and path network.

All spatial layers were transformed to the Irish Transverse Mercator
coordinate reference system, EPSG:2157, before distance, area, and
accessibility calculations were carried out. Using a projected
coordinate system ensured that spatial measurements were calculated in
metres and were consistent across the analysis.

The main statistical modelling was restricted to urban Census Small
Areas. This restriction was used because the research question focuses
on active school travel, and walking and cycling are more feasible in
urban settings where population density, school density, and
street-network connectivity are generally higher. Rural Census Small
Areas were retained for descriptive spatial comparisons, but they were
not included in the main Bayesian beta regression model.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{School Place Density:
Kriging}\label{school-place-density-kriging}

A kriging-based school-place density measure was developed to estimate
spatial variation in school-place availability across Census Small
Areas. Kriging is a geostatistical interpolation method that uses the
spatial correlation structure of observed point data to predict values
at unsampled locations. In this study, the observed point data were
school locations, and the variable interpolated was school enrolment,
used as a proxy for school-place availability. The general kriging
workflow, including empirical variograms, fitted variogram models, and
spatial prediction, follows standard geostatistical practice and is
described in Moraga's spatial statistics text
(\textbf{moraga2024spatial?}; \textbf{moraga2024kriging?}). The kriging
implementation was carried out using the \texttt{gstat} package in R
(Pebesma 2004).

School records were first converted to spatial points using their
latitude and longitude coordinates and were then transformed to the
Irish Transverse Mercator coordinate reference system, EPSG:2157. This
ensured that distance-based calculations were carried out in metres.
Records without valid coordinates or valid enrolment values were removed
because they could not contribute to the spatial interpolation. Where
multiple schools shared exactly the same coordinates, their enrolments
were summed before analysis. This ensured that co-located schools, such
as junior and senior schools on the same campus, contributed their
combined enrolment to the school-place measure. After aggregation, the
total enrolment at each unique school location was log-transformed
before fitting the kriging model.

Ordinary kriging was used to predict school-place availability at
unsampled locations. The spatial dependence in log-enrolment was first
assessed using an empirical variogram. The empirical variogram shows how
dissimilarity in log-enrolment changes as the distance between pairs of
school locations increases. A fitted variogram model was then used to
describe this spatial dependence formally and to provide the covariance
structure required for kriging prediction. In the final analysis, a
Gaussian variogram model was fitted to the empirical variogram. The
variogram was estimated using a lag width of 5 km and a maximum cutoff
distance of 200 km. Kriging predictions were made using up to 150 nearby
observed school locations for each prediction point.

Instead of predicting only at the centroid of each Census Small Area,
each Census Small Area polygon was divided into a 3 × 3 internal grid.
Grid cells were clipped to the Census Small Area boundary, and one
internal point was created within each valid clipped grid cell using a
point-on-surface approach. Kriging predictions were then obtained at
these internal prediction points and averaged to produce one raw
kriging-derived estimate for each Census Small Area. This approach
reduced dependence on a single centroid and better represented
irregularly shaped areas.

Because the kriging model was fitted on the log-enrolment scale,
predictions were transformed back to the original enrolment scale before
aggregation. A bias-corrected log-normal back-transformation was used:

\[
\widehat{E}_i =
\exp \left(
\hat{z}_i + \frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_i
\right)
\]

where \(\hat{z}_i\) is the kriging prediction on the log scale at
prediction point (i), and \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_i\) is the kriging
prediction variance at that point. This correction was used because
simply exponentiating the predicted log value would underestimate the
expected value on the original enrolment scale.

The back-transformed predictions were averaged across the internal
prediction points within each Census Small Area to produce one raw
school-place estimate per area. The corresponding kriging prediction
variance was also averaged across the prediction points to provide an
area-level measure of interpolation uncertainty.

The raw kriging estimate represents predicted school-place availability,
but it does not account for the number of children living in each area.
To account for local demand, the raw kriged value was divided by the
Census 2022 population aged 3--18 years in each Census Small Area. The
final normalised school-place density measure was expressed per 100
children aged 3--18:

\[
SchoolDensity_i =
100 \times
\frac{RawKrigedValue_i}{PopulationAged3to18_i}
\]

where \(RawKrigedValue_i\) is the raw kriging-derived school-place
estimate for Census Small Area (i), and (PopulationAged3to18\_i) is the
number of residents aged 3--18 in that area. Higher values indicate
greater predicted school-place availability relative to the local
school-age population. Lower values indicate lower predicted
availability relative to potential local demand. This normalised measure
was used as the main kriging-based school accessibility exposure in the
statistical model. The unnormalised kriging estimate was retained as a
reference measure of absolute predicted school-place availability, and
school-place density per square kilometre was also calculated as a
secondary spatial density measure.

\subsubsection{Normalisation by School-Age
Population}\label{normalisation-by-school-age-population}

The raw kriging-derived estimate represents predicted school-place
availability, but it does not account for the number of children living
in each Census Small Area. This adjustment was necessary because an area
with a high predicted number of school places may not necessarily have
high accessibility if it also has a large school-age population. To
account for local demand, the raw kriged school-place estimate was
normalised by the Census 2022 population aged 3--18 years in each Census
Small Area (Central Statistics Office 2023a).

The population aged 3--18 years was used because it approximates the
population potentially requiring access to primary and post-primary
education. Although this age range does not correspond exactly to school
attendance in every case, it provides a consistent local denominator for
comparing school-place availability across Census Small Areas.

The normalised kriging-based accessibility measure was calculated as:

\[
SchoolDensity_i =
100 \times
\frac{RawKrigedValue_i}{PopulationAged3to18_i}
\]

where \(RawKrigedValue_i\) is the raw kriging-derived school-place
estimate for Census Small Area \(i\), and \(PopulationAged3to18_i\) is
the number of residents aged 3--18 in that area. Multiplying by 100
expresses the measure as predicted school-place availability per 100
children aged 3--18, which is easier to interpret at the Census Small
Area scale than a per-child value.

Higher values indicate greater predicted school-place availability
relative to the local school-age population. Lower values indicate lower
predicted availability relative to potential local demand. This means
that the normalised measure should be interpreted as a relative
accessibility measure, rather than as the exact number of school places
physically located within each Census Small Area.

The unnormalised kriging value was retained as a reference measure of
absolute predicted school-place availability. An additional spatial
density variable, predicted school places per square kilometre, was also
calculated to describe the concentration of school places independently
of the local school-age population denominator. The normalised measure
per 100 children aged 3--18 was used as the main kriging-based
accessibility exposure in the Bayesian beta regression model.

\subsection{Travel-Time Accessibility}\label{travel-time-accessibility}

\subsubsection{Rationale and choice of
measure}\label{rationale-and-choice-of-measure}

The kriging-based measure described in the previous section estimates
how densely school places are distributed around a location, but it does
so in straight-line space and takes no account of the routes a child
would actually use. A high krigged density can occur in an area that is
in practice difficult to traverse on foot, for example where a river,
motorway, or absent crossing separates homes from nearby schools. To
capture accessibility as it is experienced along the real network, a
second measure was constructed based on travel time rather than spatial
proximity. This measure asks how many school places a child could
actually reach from their own area within a feasible walking or cycling
time, travelling along the existing road and path network.

This is a cumulative opportunity accessibility measure, one of the
standard place based approaches to measuring accessibility, in which the
number of opportunities reachable from an origin within a fixed
travel-time threshold is counted. This type of measure is well suited to
school travel, because journeys to school are short and local, and the
relevant question is not the average distance to all schools but whether
a reasonable number of school places fall within the limited time a
child would realistically spend walking or cycling. By counting
reachable places within plausible time limits, the measure reflects the
practical opportunity for active travel in each area rather than
proximity alone.

\subsubsection{The underlying method}\label{the-underlying-method}

The measure was produced using network-based routing, in which
shortest-path travel times are computed across a multimodal street
network from each origin to a set of candidate destinations. For every
origin--destination pair, the routing algorithm finds the quickest path
through the network for a given travel mode, and destinations whose
travel time falls within a chosen threshold are then treated as
reachable. This is the method underlying the accessibility calculations,
it is distinct from any particular software used to carry it out.

The routing was implemented using the R5 routing engine, accessed in R
through the r5r package. R5 efficiently computes travel times over a
network derived from OpenStreetMap data and is designed to handle large
numbers of origin--destination pairs across walking, cycling, and other
modes, which made it suitable for computing accessibility for every
Census Small Area in the study.

\subsubsection{Network and input data}\label{network-and-input-data}

The routing network was built from an OpenStreetMap extract for Ireland
{[}OSM EXTRACT SOURCE/DATE{]}. OpenStreetMap provides the road and path
geometry, including footpaths and minor links relevant to walking and
cycling, that R5 uses to construct the routable network. The origins for
the accessibility calculation were the Census Small Area prediction
points, and the destinations were the aggregated school point locations
described in the School Data section. Each destination was weighted by
its total enrolment, so that a school location contributed its number of
school places rather than counting as a single school. This ensured that
accessibility reflected the volume of school places reachable from each
area, consistent with the enrolment-weighted treatment of schools used
throughout the analysis.

\subsubsection{Travel-time computation and
thresholds}\label{travel-time-computation-and-thresholds}

Travel times were computed separately for walking and for cycling, using
mode-specific speeds of {[}will add walking speed{]} for walking and
{[}will add cycling speed{]} for cycling. Walking accessibility was
evaluated at thresholds of 10, 20, and 30 minutes, and cycling
accessibility at thresholds of 5, 10, 15, and 20 minutes. These
thresholds were chosen to span the range of times a child might
plausibly spend travelling actively to school, from short journeys up to
a realistic upper limit. For each origin and each threshold, the number
of reachable school places was obtained by summing the
enrolment-weighted destinations whose network travel time fell within
the threshold.

The 30-minute walking measure was carried forward as the primary
travel-time exposure in the statistical model. Thirty minutes was
treated as the most plausible upper bound for a routine walk to school,
long enough to capture the realistic extent of walking journeys while
remaining within the time a daily school commute on foot would
reasonably take. The shorter walking thresholds and the cycling
thresholds were retained for descriptive comparison but were not used as
the main model exposure.

\#\#\#Normalisation and transformation

As with the kriging measure, the raw count of reachable school places
reflects the supply of places without reference to the number of
children competing for them. To express accessibility relative to local
demand, the count of reachable places for each Census Small Area was
divided by its Census 2022 population aged 3--18 and expressed per 100
children:

\[
WalkAccess_i = 100 \times \frac{ReachablePlaces_i}{PopulationAged3to18_i}
\]

where \(ReachablePlaces_i\) is the enrolment-weighted number of school
places reachable within 30 minutes of walking from Census Small Area
\(i\), and \(PopulationAged3to18_i\) is the number of residents aged
3--18 in that area. This matches the per-100-children scaling used for
the kriging-based measure, so that both accessibility measures are
expressed on a comparable demand-adjusted basis.

The distribution of reachable-place counts was strongly right-skewed,
because a small number of dense urban areas could reach many thousands
of school places while peripheral areas could reach very few. To prevent
these extreme values from dominating the model and to allow the
predictor to vary on a multiplicative rather than additive scale, the
normalised measure was log-transformed using a base-10 logarithm with an
offset of one:

\[
WalkAccessLog_i = \log_{10}\left(WalkAccess_i + 1\right)
\]

The offset of one ensured that areas able to reach no school places
within the threshold remained defined after transformation. This
log-transformed, demand-adjusted variable was used as the final
travel-time accessibility exposure in the Bayesian beta regression
model.

\subsection{Demographic Variables: ILR
Transformation}\label{demographic-variables-ilr-transformation}

Several demographic variables derived from Census 2022 Small Area
Population Statistics were included to adjust for differences in
population structure across Census Small Areas. These variables describe
age structure, household car availability, and educational attainment.
Because these variables are expressed as percentages within groups, they
are compositional: the parts represent shares of a whole and are
therefore constrained to sum to a fixed total. Standard regression
models can give unstable or misleading results when compositional
variables are included directly, because an increase in one component
necessarily implies a decrease in at least one other component
(Aitchison 1986).

Three demographic compositions were constructed. Age composition was
grouped into five categories: children aged 0--12, young people aged
13--24, adults aged 25--44, adults aged 45--74, and adults aged 75 and
over. Household car availability was grouped into households with no
car, one car, and two or more cars. Educational attainment was grouped
into no formal or primary education, secondary education, and tertiary
education. The percentage still in education was retained as a separate
demographic covariate, rather than being included in the educational
attainment composition, because it was calculated using a different
denominator.

To account for the compositional nature of these variables, the
isometric log-ratio transformation was applied to each composition. The
ILR transformation converts a composition with \(D\) parts into
\(D - 1\) real-valued coordinates that can be used in standard
regression models (Egozcue et al. 2003). These coordinates represent
balances between groups of compositional parts rather than isolated
changes in individual percentages. For example, an ILR coordinate for
age composition may represent a contrast between younger and older age
groups, rather than the independent effect of one age group alone.

For a composition \(\mathbf{x}_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2}, ..., x_{iD})\) in
Census Small Area (i), the transformation can be written generally as:

\[
\mathbf{z}_i = ilr(\mathbf{x}_i)
\]

where \(\mathbf{z}_i\) is the set of (D - 1) ILR coordinates. These
transformed coordinates are unconstrained and can therefore be included
as regression covariates without the perfect collinearity that would
arise from including all raw percentage components directly.

Exact zero values cannot be used directly in log-ratio transformations
because the logarithm of zero is undefined. Before applying the ILR
transformation, exact zero percentages were therefore replaced with a
small positive value of 0.001. This allowed the transformation to be
computed while making only a minimal change to the original percentage
values.

The ILR transformation was implemented in R using the
\texttt{compositions} package (Boogaart and Tolosana-Delgado 2008). The
resulting ILR-transformed demographic variables were included as
covariates in the Bayesian beta regression model. This allowed the model
to adjust for demographic differences between urban Census Small Areas
while avoiding the collinearity problems associated with raw percentage
variables. The original percentage variables were retained for
descriptive summaries, but the ILR-transformed variables were used in
the regression model.

\subsection{Statistical Model: Bayesian Beta
Regression}\label{statistical-model-bayesian-beta-regression}

The main statistical model was used to estimate the association between
school accessibility and active school travel in urban Census Small
Areas. The outcome variable was the proportion of pupils travelling to
school by active modes, defined as walking or cycling. Because this
outcome is continuous and bounded between 0 and 1, beta regression was
used. Beta regression is appropriate for modelling rates and proportions
that lie within the open interval ((0, 1)), and allows the expected
value of the outcome to be related to explanatory variables through a
regression structure (Ferrari and Cribari-Neto 2004).

The analysis was restricted to urban Census Small Areas because the main
research question focuses on active school travel in settings where
walking and cycling are more feasible. Before modelling, Census Small
Areas with missing required model inputs were removed. In addition,
areas with total population greater than 900 and areas with fewer than
five residents aged 3--18 were excluded. These exclusions were applied
to reduce the influence of unusually large Census Small Areas and
unstable school-age population denominators. The final urban modelling
dataset therefore included Census Small Areas with valid active-travel
outcomes, valid school accessibility measures, and sufficient population
information.

The active-travel percentage was converted to a proportion before
modelling. Since the beta distribution is defined only on the open
interval ((0, 1)), any exact zero or one values were adjusted slightly
so that all observations lay strictly within this interval. This type of
transformation is commonly used when applying beta regression to
proportional outcomes that may include boundary values (Smithson and
Verkuilen 2006).

The model was fitted using a Bayesian beta regression framework. For
Census Small Area (i), the outcome was modelled as:

\[
y_i \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\mu_i, \phi)
\]

where \(y_i\) is the active-travel proportion, \(\mu_i\) is the expected
active-travel proportion, and \(\phi\) is the beta precision parameter.
Under this parameterisation, \(E(y_i) = \mu_i\). Larger values of
\(\phi\) indicate that observations are more tightly concentrated around
the expected proportion, while smaller values indicate greater
variability.

A logit link function was used to relate the expected active-travel
proportion to the explanatory variables:

\[
\mathrm{logit}(\mu_i) =
\alpha +
\sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j Z_{ij}
+
f_1(K_i)
+
f_2(D_i)
\]

where \(\alpha\) is the intercept, \(Z_{ij}\) represents the set of
standardised demographic covariates for Census Small Area \(i\), \(K_i\)
is the normalised kriging-based school-place density measure, and
\(D_i\) is the secondary spatial density measure of predicted school
places per square kilometre. The demographic covariates included the
ILR-transformed age composition, household car-availability composition,
and educational-attainment composition, together with the additional
percentage still in education covariate where included. These variables
were used to adjust for differences in demographic structure across
urban Census Small Areas.

The terms \(f_1(K_i)\) and \(f_2(D_i)\) represent natural cubic spline
functions. Natural cubic splines were used because the relationship
between school accessibility and active travel may not be linear. For
example, increases in school accessibility may be strongly associated
with active travel at low levels of accessibility, but the association
may become weaker or flatten at higher levels. Using splines allows the
model to capture this type of non-linear relationship without assuming a
single constant effect across the full range of the accessibility
variables. Three degrees of freedom were used for each spline, providing
a flexible but controlled curve and reducing the risk of overfitting.

The normalised kriging-based accessibility variable was expressed as
predicted school-place availability per 100 children aged 3--18. This
variable captures school-place availability relative to local school-age
population demand. The additional school-place density per square
kilometre variable was included to capture the spatial concentration of
predicted school places independently of the local child population
denominator. Including both variables allowed the model to distinguish
between relative school-place availability and broader spatial
concentration of school places.

To account for differences in the reliability of active-travel
percentages across Census Small Areas, the model used weights based on
the active-travel denominator. Census Small Areas with larger numbers of
pupils with a stated travel mode provide more stable estimates of
active-travel proportion than areas with smaller denominators. The
weights were calculated as the square root of the active-travel
denominator and then normalised to have an average value of one in the
urban modelling sample. This gave more influence to areas with more
reliable active-travel estimates while avoiding excessive dominance by
the largest areas.

Weakly informative priors were used for the model parameters. Regression
coefficients were assigned normal priors centred at zero, which
regularised the model by discouraging unrealistically large effects
while still allowing the data to inform the posterior estimates. The
intercept was assigned a wider normal prior on the logit scale. The beta
precision parameter \(\phi\) was assigned a positive prior to ensure
that it remained greater than zero. These priors were chosen to provide
regularisation without imposing strong assumptions about the direction
or size of the accessibility effects.

The model was fitted using the \texttt{brms} package in R, which fits
Bayesian regression models using Stan (Bürkner 2017). Posterior sampling
was carried out using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn
Sampler, as implemented in Stan (Hoffman and Gelman 2014). Four Markov
chains were run, and convergence was assessed using standard Bayesian
diagnostics, including \(\hat{R}\), effective sample size, trace plots,
divergent transitions, and posterior predictive checks. Values of
\(\hat{R}\) close to 1 and adequate effective sample sizes were taken as
evidence that the chains had mixed well (Vehtari et al. 2021).

Because spline coefficients are not directly interpretable as simple
one-unit changes, the results were interpreted using posterior
predictions and marginal effect plots on the response scale. These plots
show how the expected active-travel percentage changes across the
observed range of the accessibility variables. Interpretation was based
on posterior means and credible intervals, rather than only on raw
regression coefficients. Posterior predictive checks were used to assess
whether the fitted model could reproduce the observed distribution of
active travel.

\subsubsection{Walking Accessibility}\label{walking-accessibility}

This section describes the walking accessibility measure across Census
Small Areas (CSAs). The measure counts how many school places can be
reached on foot within 30 minutes from each CSA, expressed per 100
children aged 3--18. It is based on routing-based travel time, which
uses the real road and path network rather than straight-line distance,
so it reflects the routes a child would actually use and captures the
effect of any barriers in the network. Figure X maps the walking
accessibility measure, with its distribution shown alongside. The island
of Ireland boundary is drawn beneath the map for geographical context.
The values are strongly right-skewed: a small number of dense urban CSAs
can reach many thousands of school places, while most CSAs reach far
fewer. The median walking accessibility is about 920 places per 100
children, but the highest values reach into the tens of thousands.
Because of this skew, the map colour scale uses a square-root
transformation so that differences at the lower end of the distribution
remain visible, which a linear scale would hide. The map shows that
walking accessibility is highest in and around the larger urban areas,
where schools and street networks are dense, and decreases towards the
edges of towns and along the coast. Some CSAs on peninsulas, islands,
and estuary edges have very low or zero walking accessibility even
though schools are not far away in straight-line distance. This pattern
is important for the comparison that follows, because it suggests that
barriers in the network, rather than distance alone, affect how
reachable schools really are. The distribution shown beside the map
confirms the strong right skew, including a group of CSAs at or near
zero walking accessibility.

\subsubsection{Effect of Accessibility on Active
Travel}\label{effect-of-accessibility-on-active-travel}

This section shows how the predicted percentage of active travel changes
across the range of each accessibility measure, with all other
covariates held at their average. Because the spline coefficients are
not directly interpretable, these marginal-effect curves give a clearer
picture of each measure's effect on the response scale. Figure X shows
the two curves. The two measures entered their models on different
scales (the kriging measure on its original scale and the walking
measure on a log10 scale), so the curves should be read individually
rather than compared by their position on the horizontal axis. They can,
however, be compared on the vertical axis, since both predict the same
outcome. The kriging curve (left) is gentle and slightly non-monotonic.
Predicted active travel changes only modestly across the range of kriged
density, moving within a band of roughly 41\% to 44\%, and the credible
interval is wide. This indicates that kriged school-place density has
only a small and uncertain association with active travel once
demographic differences are accounted for. The walking curve (right) is
much steeper and has a clear S-shape. Predicted active travel rises from
around 15\% at the lowest levels of walking accessibility to around 60\%
at the highest, and the credible interval is narrow throughout. This
indicates a strong and fairly certain association: areas where more
school places can actually be reached on foot have substantially higher
active travel. Comparing the two curves on the vertical axis, the
walking measure is associated with a much larger change in active travel
than the kriging measure, and with greater certainty. This is consistent
with the severance pattern described earlier: what matters for active
travel is not simply how close schools are in straight-line terms, but
whether they can actually be reached on foot. The walking measure
captures this directly, which may explain why it is more strongly
associated with active travel than the straight-line kriging measure.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}\label{interpretation-of-results}

The central finding of this study is that how schools are reached
matters more than how close they are in straight-line terms. The two
accessibility measures agreed on average but diverged in many individual
CSAs, and the areas where they diverged most pointed consistently to
severance, where physical barriers such as motorways, rivers, and water
separate an area from schools that are nearby as the crow flies. The
Jacob's Island example in Cork illustrates this clearly: schools are
close in straight-line distance, but the estuary and the surrounding
road network mean none can be reached within a 30-minute walk. This
interpretation is supported by the statistical model. The walking
accessibility measure, which is based on actual network travel time, had
a strong and fairly certain association with active travel, while the
kriged density measure, which is based on straight-line distance, had
only a small and uncertain association. Taken together, the comparison
and the model suggest that straight-line measures of school-place
availability can overstate how reachable schools really are, and that
network-based measures capture the practical opportunity for active
travel more faithfully. These findings connect back to the ``postcode
lottery'' idea that motivated the study. They suggest that unequal
opportunities for active school travel are not only about where schools
are located, but also about whether the local network allows children to
reach them on foot. Two areas with similar straight-line access to
schools can have very different walking opportunities if one is cut off
by a barrier. This points to the importance of local infrastructure,
such as pedestrian crossings and routes across or around barriers, in
supporting active school travel, alongside the broader distribution of
school places.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, the outcome data are
restricted by the way school travel information is released in official
statistics. The Census data provide aggregated travel-to-education
counts for Census Small Areas, rather than individual-level journeys
between home and school. As a result, the analysis cannot observe the
actual school attended by each pupil, the exact route taken, or whether
pupils travel to primary or post-primary schools. The model therefore
estimates area-level associations between school accessibility and
active travel, rather than individual travel behaviour. This means that
the results should be interpreted as evidence of spatial association,
not as proof that improved accessibility directly causes higher walking
or cycling rates.

Second, the analysis combines school accessibility measures with an
outcome that is not fully separated by school type in the main Small
Area-level data. Primary and post-primary pupils may have different
travel distances, school-choice patterns, and active travel
opportunities. For example, younger children may be more dependent on
nearby primary schools, while secondary students may travel further
because post-primary schools are fewer and more specialised. Future work
should therefore repeat the analysis separately for primary and
post-primary schools where the data allow. However, this may be limited
by the availability of travel-mode outcomes disaggregated by school type
at fine spatial scales.

Third, the school accessibility measures use enrolment as a proxy for
school places. Enrolment provides a consistent measure of the relative
size and spatial distribution of schools, but it does not measure spare
capacity, catchment areas, admissions policies, or whether a place is
realistically available to a child living in a particular area. This
limitation is especially relevant where schools are oversubscribed or
where school choice is shaped by factors other than distance.

Fourth, the kriging-based accessibility measure captures the spatial
distribution of school places, but it does not account for barriers in
the transport network, route safety, traffic conditions, or the quality
of walking and cycling infrastructure. The routing-based travel-time
measure addresses this limitation more directly by using the road and
path network, but it still depends on assumptions about walking and
cycling speeds and on the completeness and accuracy of OpenStreetMap
data.

Finally, the workflow could be repeated using settlement-level data as a
complementary analysis. Settlement-level travel tables can describe
travel behaviour for whole towns or urban areas, rather than only Census
Small Areas. This may provide a broader view of how school provision and
active travel relate at the town scale, and could be useful for
comparing with previous work on Irish settlements. However,
settlement-level data are less spatially detailed than Census Small Area
data, so they would be better suited as a complementary robustness or
contextual analysis rather than a replacement for the main local-scale
model.

\subsection{Comparison with the
article}\label{comparison-with-the-article}

This study was motivated by Mancini's work on adolescent active
transport in Ireland, which argued that differences in active travel
between areas are linked to the geography of school provision rather
than simply to population size (Mancini 2025, 2024b). The findings here
are broadly consistent with that argument, but they add a spatial
dimension to it. Where Mancini focused on the presence and type of
school provision, this study shows that even where school provision
appears adequate in straight-line terms, active travel can still be
limited if schools are not reachable on foot through the local network.
In this sense, the present study complements rather than contradicts the
earlier work. It supports the idea that active school travel is shaped
by the geography of provision, while highlighting that the connectivity
of the network is an additional and important part of that geography.
The ``postcode lottery'' of active travel may therefore depend not only
on whether school places exist nearby, but on whether the surrounding
network allows children to walk to them. A practical difference is that
this study worked at the finer Census Small Area scale rather than the
settlement level, which made it possible to detect the local severance
effects that a coarser analysis would miss.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

This study examined whether school-place accessibility is associated
with active school travel across Ireland, using two complementary
measures: a kriging-based measure of school-place density based on
straight-line distance, and a walking accessibility measure based on
actual network travel time. By comparing the two measures across Census
Small Areas and modelling their association with active travel, the
study aimed to test the idea that opportunities for walking and cycling
to school may depend on where children live. The two measures agreed on
average but diverged in many individual areas. These divergences
consistently reflected severance, where barriers such as motorways,
rivers, and water cut an area off from schools that are nearby in
straight-line terms, as illustrated by the Jacob's Island example in
Cork. The statistical model reinforced this picture: the walking
accessibility measure had a strong and fairly certain association with
active travel, while the straight-line kriging measure had only a small
and uncertain association. Taken together, these results suggest that
what matters most for active school travel is not how close schools are
in straight-line terms, but whether they can actually be reached on
foot. This has practical implications for how school accessibility is
measured and for how active travel is supported: network-based measures
capture the real opportunity for walking more faithfully than
straight-line measures, and local infrastructure that overcomes barriers
may be as important as the broader distribution of school places. Future
work could extend the analysis by separating primary and post-primary
schools, by including cycling accessibility computed with the same
network method, and by linking these measures to local infrastructure
data to identify where targeted improvements would most improve active
school travel.
